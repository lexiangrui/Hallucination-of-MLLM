\subsection{Analysis}

\subsubsection{Cross-Model Comparison}
\label{sec:pope_cross_model_comparison}



The four models exhibit distinct decision styles,
characterized by hallucination control (HR) and response bias (Yes Ratio).
\Cref{fig:pope_hr_bar} visualizes the HR of all four models
across the three splits.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig1_pope_hr_bar.pdf}
  \caption{Hallucination rates of four MLLMs
    across the three POPE splits
    (Random, Popular, Adversarial).}
  \label{fig:pope_hr_bar}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B} achieves the lowest HR on every split
(0.30\%, 2.40\%, 4.80\%)
with a stable Yes Ratio ($\leq$0.47),
suggesting a strong visual encoder
paired with a balanced decision style.

\textbf{Gemini 2.5 Flash} shows balanced Precision--Recall
and is the only model whose HR \emph{decreases}
from Popular (5.70\%) to Adversarial (5.10\%),
indicating greater robustness
to co-occurrence distractors (Adversarial)
than to global frequency bias (Popular).

\textbf{Qwen3.5-35B-A3B} attains the highest Recall on every split
($\geq$0.87)
but at the cost of the panel's highest Yes Ratio
(peaking at 0.529 on Adversarial)
and worst Adversarial HR (8.90\%),
a pattern consistent with a permissive decision style.

\textbf{GPT-5.4-mini} adopts the opposite, conservative strategy:
the lowest Yes Ratio (0.414 on Random)
suppresses HR on easier splits
but produces the steepest cross-split degradation
(Adversarial HR = 7.80\%, F1 = 0.8248, both panel-worst),
as the ``default to No'' shortcut fails
under adversarial pressure.

\subsubsection{Three Splits Detect Three Error Mechanisms}
\label{sec:pope_difficulty}

\textbf{Monotonic difficulty gradient.}
From Random to Popular to Adversarial,
the Hallucination Rate of most models increases monotonically
(\Cref{tab:pope_results,fig:pope_hr_bar}),
consistent with POPE's three-tier negative-sampling design.
Beyond a difficulty ranking,
however, the three splits isolate
three qualitatively distinct error mechanisms
that map onto the faithfulness taxonomy.

\textbf{Random split: object-level perception.}
Negative objects are sampled uniformly,
so most candidates are visually unrelated to the scene
(e.g., asking about a car in a kitchen photo).
All four models achieve HR between 0.30\% and 1.10\%,
indicating that the baseline ability to reject obviously absent objects
is near saturation.
This split therefore measures raw object-level visual perception,
with negligible interference from language priors.

\textbf{Popular split: global frequency bias.}
Negative objects are drawn from the top-$K$ most frequent categories
in the entire COCO dataset,
which are globally common regardless of scene context,
but absent from the specific image.
HR rises sharply to 2.40\%--6.20\%,
roughly 5--8 times the Random level.
The surge reflects the high-frequency language prior:
when the visual evidence is ambiguous,
models tend to affirm frequently occurring categories,
falling back on the statistical distribution
learned during pretraining.
Case~1 in \Cref{sec:case1_pope} provides a direct example.

\textbf{Adversarial split: high-frequency co-occurrence interference.}
Negative objects are selected based on COCO training co-occurrence statistics,
co-occurring frequently with objects present in the image
but absent from the specific image.
HR rises further to 4.80\%--8.90\%
and the model is now disrupted by the co-occurrence statistics
from its training data:
the conditional co-occurrence probability learned in the language module
overrides visual perception.
Case~2 in \Cref{sec:case2_pope}
shows that this is the hardest of the three failure modes,
because the underlying cause, the strength of the co-occurrence prior
exceeding that of the visual signal, is
not addressable by scale alone.

\subsubsection{Root Cause: The Confounding Effects of Dual Modality Priors}
\label{sec:pope_dual_priors}

\textbf{The architectural asymmetry of MLLMs.}
Current mainstream MLLMs all adopt the modular architecture of
``vision encoder + projector + LLM backbone'':
the vision encoder (typically a CLIP or SigLIP ViT)
converts an image into feature vectors,
which an MLP projector then maps
into the LLM's word embedding space;
the resulting visual tokens are concatenated with text tokens
and fed into the LLM for autoregressive generation.
This architecture harbors a fundamental asymmetry:
the parameter count and pretraining data scale of the LLM backbone
far exceed those of the visual pathway.
Taking Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B as an example,
its vision encoder is SigLIP2-SO-400M (400M parameters)
while the language backbone contains 235B total parameters (22B active)---%
a difference of roughly two orders of magnitude
(588$\times$ in total parameters) \citep{baiQwen3VLTechnical2025}.
In the shared self-attention computation
where visual and text tokens interact,
this scale disparity grants the language modality
a structural advantage at the parameter level.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{causalmm_scm.png}
  \caption{Structural Causal Model (SCM) of MLLM inference proposed by CausalMM.
    Blue nodes ($V$, $L$) represent attention mechanisms;
    red nodes ($P_V$, $P_L$) are modality priors acting as confounders;
    gray nodes are image ($I$), visual embeddings ($T_V$),
    text embeddings ($T_L$), and output ($O$).
    Red paths indicate the confounding effects of $P_V$ and $P_L$
    on attention and output.
    Source: \citet{zhouMitigatingModalityPrior2025}, ICLR~2025.}
  \label{fig:causalmm_scm}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Two independent confounding factors.}
\citet{zhouMitigatingModalityPrior2025}
demonstrated through a structural causal model in CausalMM (ICLR~2025)
that this architectural asymmetry introduces
two independent confounding factors
(\Cref{fig:causalmm_scm}):
(1)~the \textbf{visual modality prior $P_V$},
originating from the parametric bias of the vision encoder---%
the training distribution of CLIP/SigLIP
determines the encoder's sensitivity or blindness
to specific visual patterns;
and (2)~the \textbf{linguistic modality prior $P_L$},
originating from the statistical co-occurrence regularities
that the LLM has acquired
from billions of tokens of text-only pretraining.
These two priors influence the final output
through distinct attention pathways:
$P_V$ distorts the attention distribution within the vision encoder,
thereby contaminating the visual embeddings passed to the LLM;
$P_L$ activates pathways in the LLM's multi-head self-attention
that are consistent with linguistic statistics,
and its effect is most pronounced at the shallow-to-middle layers
where visual tokens first interact with text tokens.


\textbf{A unified causal account of the three POPE error mechanisms.}
Returning to the experimental results (\Cref{sec:pope_difficulty}),
the three error patterns correspond precisely
to how these two priors interact under different conditions.
In the Random split,
negative objects are unrelated to the scene
and $P_L$ is barely engaged;
the residual hallucination rate of 0.30\%--1.10\%
stems from the baseline bias of $P_V$ alone,
reflecting the upper bound of the vision encoder's perceptual capacity.
The Popular split represents the $P_L$ high-frequency prior in its purest form---%
backpack, as a globally frequent category in COCO,
is selected as a negative object regardless of image context.
When visual evidence is ambiguous,
models tend to affirm such high-frequency words,
as illustrated by Case~1
where all four models incorrectly assert the presence of a backpack,
causing the hallucination rate to surge to 2.40\%--6.20\%.
The Adversarial split exhibits a joint failure of $P_V$ and $P_L$:
$P_L$'s co-occurrence prior makes bear a distractor
because it co-occurs frequently with person and book in the training statistics,
while $P_V$ fails to provide sufficiently fine-grained visual discrimination
to counteract this co-occurrence bias.
Their combined effect, as shown in Case~2,
causes all four models to merge bear with teddy bear,
producing the highest hallucination rate (4.80\%--8.90\%).
This also explains why increasing model scale
cannot resolve Adversarial hallucinations
(Qwen3-VL-235B still exhibits 4.80\%)---%
scaling primarily strengthens $P_L$'s statistical precision,
and the co-occurrence prior is precisely
what gets amplified with more training data,
rather than being mitigated by larger models.
\subsection{Failure Case Analysis}

\subsubsection{Case 1: Global Frequency Bias (Popular)}
\label{sec:case1_pope}

This case illustrates
the global frequency bias mechanism
isolated by the Popular split (\Cref{sec:pope_difficulty}).
\Cref{tab:pope_case1,fig:pope_case1} present a sample from the Popular split.
The image shows
a person eating a sandwich and a hot dog outdoors.
The question asks whether there is a backpack in the image,
and the ground truth is No.
All four models answer Yes.

The root cause is global frequency bias.
``Backpack'' is one of the most frequently occurring categories
in the COCO dataset
\citep{linMicrosoftCOCOCommon2015},
and was therefore selected as a negative sample
by the Popular sampling strategy,
regardless of the image context.
The language module has learned a high prior probability
for such frequently occurring categories during pretraining;
when the visual signal is ambiguous,
this high-frequency prior overrides visual perception,
causing the model to produce an affirmative answer.
The Popular split exploits this:
models mistake the statistical distribution of frequent categories
in the training data for visual evidence.

\subsubsection{Case 2: High-frequency Co-occurrence Interference (Adversarial)}
\label{sec:case2_pope}

This case illustrates
the high-frequency co-occurrence interference mechanism
identified in \Cref{sec:pope_difficulty}.
\Cref{tab:pope_case2,fig:pope_case2} present a sample from the Adversarial split.
The image contains
a teddy bear, a person, and a book.
The question asks whether there is a bear in the image,
and the ground truth is No.
All four models answer Yes,
indicating a systematic error rather than an individual model deficiency.

The root cause is high-frequency co-occurrence interference.
According to the COCO training co-occurrence statistics,
``bear'' co-occurs frequently
with objects present in the image (``person,'' ``book''),
and was therefore selected as a negative sample
by the Adversarial sampling strategy.
The language module has learned this co-occurrence pattern,
and the conditional co-occurrence probability
creates a strong affirmative bias;
meanwhile, the vision encoder lacks sufficient fine-grained discrimination
between teddy bear and bear
to counteract this bias through the visual signal.
Their combined effect causes all four models to answer Yes.
The Adversarial split exploits a different dynamic:
the joint failure of $P_L$'s co-occurrence prior
and $P_V$'s perceptual limitation
produces a higher hallucination rate
than either Random or Popular.
