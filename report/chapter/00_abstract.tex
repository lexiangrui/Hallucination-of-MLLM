\begin{abstract}
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities
in visual question answering and image-text reasoning,
yet they remain susceptible to hallucinations---outputs
that are inconsistent with the visual input,
contradict established world knowledge,
or contain flaws in the reasoning process.
This report presents a systematic framework
for detecting and evaluating hallucinations in MLLMs.
I adopt a three-category taxonomy
covering faithfulness, factuality, and logical hallucinations,
and evaluate four representative models
(GPT-5.4-mini, Gemini~2.5~Flash,
Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B)
across three benchmarks
(POPE, MathVista, VQA-RAD)
using two complementary detection methods:
a rule-based pipeline for binary probing
and an MLLM-Judge protocol
following the MMHal-Bench 0--6 Likert scale,
with GPT-5.5 as the primary judge.
Three findings emerge.
(1)~Model rankings reverse across task domains,
and a two-axis view
of visual-encoder capacity and response-bias calibration
explains both the panel ordering on POPE
and the ranking shifts on MathVista and VQA-RAD.
(2)~Chain-of-Thought prompting is baseline-dependent:
it substantially reduces hallucinations in weaker-baseline models
(GPT-5.4-mini $-$10.7\,pp, Gemini~2.5~Flash $-$6.7\,pp)
but exacerbates them in stronger-baseline ones
(Qwen3-VL $+$7.7\,pp),
through two specifically identified mechanisms---
an \emph{exposure effect}
(latent perceptual errors made visible by reasoning chains)
and a \emph{commitment error}
(snowballed elaboration around an early wrong premise).
(3)~In medical VQA,
faithfulness hallucinations account for over 96\% of all hallucinations,
and a recurring confident-but-wrong failure mode---models
recite domain conventions accurately
yet localize anatomical findings incorrectly---demonstrates
that fluent medical knowledge does not entail medical visual perception.
Five independent error analyses
(human alignment with Cohen's $\kappa = 0.70$,
cross-judge consistency between GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus~4.7 with $\kappa = 0.78$,
threshold sensitivity, length-bias assessment,
and cross-model overlap)
confirm the robustness of the evaluation framework
and trace the principal residual judge--human disagreement
to a coupling of the informativeness and hallucination dimensions
in the underlying scoring rubric.
\end{abstract}
