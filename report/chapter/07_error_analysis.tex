\section{Error Analysis}

\subsection{Human Alignment Verification (Human-as-Judge)}

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Setup.}
This experiment examines the agreement
between the GPT-5.5 Judge's hallucination judgments
and those of a human annotator,
following the human alignment methodology
of \citet{zhouOmniI2CHolisticBenchmark2026}.
Evaluation metrics include Accuracy, F1, Cohen's $\kappa$
(chance-corrected agreement),
and Type Agreement
(the proportion of matching hallucination type labels
among samples that both human and judge classify as hallucinated).
$\kappa$ values are interpreted
following the scale of \citet{landisKoch1977}:
$<$0.20 slight, 0.21--0.40 fair, 0.41--0.60 moderate,
0.61--0.80 substantial, 0.81--1.00 almost perfect.

Forty samples are drawn from MathVista testmini CoT results
via stratified sampling
(10 per model, approximately 5 hallucinated and 5 non-hallucinated each,
seed 42),
prioritizing coverage of all three hallucination types
within the hallucinated group.
The annotation interface displays each image,
question, model response, and reference answer.
The annotator judges whether hallucination is present
and, if so, labels the type
(faithfulness, factuality, or logical).
A single-blind design
(model name and judge verdict hidden)
is used to avoid annotation bias.
\Cref{fig:annotate_interface} shows the annotation interface.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{annotate.png}
  \caption{Human annotation interface used in the alignment experiment.
    Each screen shows the image, question, model response,
    and reference answer,
    with model name and judge verdict hidden to avoid bias.}
  \label{fig:annotate_interface}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Strong overall alignment with no missed hallucinations.}
Overall alignment is substantial ($\kappa = 0.70$),
and---critically for the validity of the automated pipeline---the
MLLM Judge does not miss any human-identified hallucination
(Recall = 100\%).
The error profile is asymmetric:
Precision = 70\% indicates 6 over-judgments
(human says non-hallucinated, judge says hallucinated)
but zero under-judgments.
For an automated hallucination detector
this is the preferable failure mode,
since downstream uses can re-examine flagged cases
but cannot recover from silently missed ones.
On the per-model breakdown,
GPT-5.4-mini and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B align perfectly
on this 10-sample subset
($\kappa = 1.0$),
reflecting clear and stable hallucination patterns
in their responses;
the per-model $\kappa$ values
are statistically loose at $N=10$ and should be read as descriptive.
Gemini 2.5 Flash has the worst alignment ($\kappa = 0.20$),
with 4 of the 6 over-judgments attributed to this model.

\textbf{Root cause: rubric coupling.}
All six over-judgments share a single error pattern:
age-estimation questions
where the model produces a reasonable refusal
(e.g., ``cannot determine age from appearance'')
that the MLLM Judge misclassifies as logical hallucination.
The root cause lies in the coupling
of the ``informativeness'' and ``hallucination'' dimensions
in the MMHal-Bench scoring rubric:
score 3 is the only slot matching
``low information + no hallucination,''
but because the model's refusal contains some information
(explaining the reason for refusal),
the judge tends to assign scores of 1--2,
pushing the sample into the ``hallucinated'' category.
The scoring rubric therefore lacks
an intermediate slot for ``moderately informative and hallucination-free''
responses,
which points to a concrete refinement target
(an extra moderate-information no-hallucination bin)
should the MMHal-Bench rubric be revised for future use.
One caveat: because the 40-sample stratified pool
favors hallucinated cases over benign refusals,
the prevalence of this single pattern
should be re-examined on a larger and less stratified sample
before generalizing.

\subsection{Judge Model Consistency Check}

\textbf{Setup.}
This experiment examines whether evaluation conclusions
depend on the choice of judge model,
by comparing GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.7
on the same sample set.
One hundred samples are randomly drawn
from MathVista testmini (seed 42),
yielding 100 responses per evaluated model (400 total).
Both judges use identical system prompts
(MMHal-Bench 0--6 rubric, five few-shot examples,
hallucination type classification).

\Cref{tab:judge_consistency} reports the results;
the ``Model'' column refers to the model whose responses are being judged,
not to the judge.

\textbf{Substantial agreement with preserved ranking.}
The two judges show substantial overall agreement
($\kappa = 0.776$, Spearman $\rho = 0.740$),
and the relative model ranking is preserved under both:
in each judge, both Qwen models rank best,
GPT-5.4-mini sits in the middle,
and Gemini 2.5 Flash is the weakest.
The largest per-model divergence
falls on Gemini ($\kappa = 0.591$):
Claude rates Gemini's HR 10\,pp lower than GPT-5.5 does,
indicating that Gemini's verbose responses
sit closer to the disagreement boundary
between the two judges' calibration of ``informative but wrong.''
GPT-5.4-mini is by contrast the most stable ($\kappa = 0.98$),
likely because its often single-token Direct responses
leave little room for judge interpretation.
These conclusions are based on two frontier closed-source judges
and should be re-examined
when an open-source or domain-specific judge becomes available.

\subsection{Threshold Sensitivity Analysis}

\textbf{Setup.}
This analysis examines how sensitive
the Hallucination Rate is to the threshold $T = 3$.
For each threshold $T$ from 0 to 6,
HR($T$) is computed as $N_{\text{score}<T} / N_{\text{total}}$.

\Cref{tab:threshold_sensitivity} reports the results
for three representative thresholds.

  \centering
  \caption{Threshold sensitivity (MathVista Direct).}
  \label{tab:threshold_sensitivity}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l ccc cc}

    Model & HR($T$=2) & HR($T$=3) & HR($T$=4)
      & $\Delta(3{\to}2)$ & $\Delta(4{\to}3)$ \\
    \midrule
    GPT-5.4-mini     & 18.1\% & 45.1\% & 45.3\% & $-$27.0\,pp & $+$0.2\,pp \\
    Gemini 2.5 Flash & 1.2\%  & 34.3\% & 34.6\% & $-$33.1\,pp & $+$0.3\,pp \\
    Qwen3.5-35B      & 0.8\%  & 13.3\% & 13.3\% & $-$12.5\,pp & $+$0.0\,pp \\
    Qwen3-VL-235B    & 2.1\%  & 16.3\% & 16.4\% & $-$14.2\,pp & $+$0.1\,pp \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig3_threshold_sensitivity_line.pdf}
  \caption{Hallucination rate as a function of threshold $T$
    (MathVista Direct).
    The vertical dashed line marks the official threshold $T = 3$.}
  \label{fig:threshold_sensitivity}

\textbf{Bimodal scores; ranking robust to threshold.}
HR($T$=3) and HR($T$=4) are nearly identical
(difference $<$0.3\,pp),
which implies that very few samples receive score 3
and the judge's scores follow a clear bimodal distribution.
The largest jump occurs between $T$=2 and $T$=3
(12.5--33.1\,pp),
indicating a concentration of samples at score 2.
The model ranking is fully preserved
across all three thresholds,
so the core conclusions are insensitive to the threshold choice.

\subsection{Length Bias Analysis}

\textbf{Setup.}
This analysis examines whether the MLLM Judge
exhibits a systematic length bias.
Spearman correlation coefficients
between response length (word count) and judge score
are computed for all MathVista samples
in both Direct and CoT modes.
Each row also reports the median length per mode
and the mean score across length quartiles
(Q1 = shortest, Q4 = longest).

\Cref{tab:length_bias} reports the results.

    and judge score (MathVista).
    Q1$\to$Q4 columns give the mean score

      & 4.38$\to$4.92 & 5.32$\to$4.32 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{No uniform length bias; CoT shifts the sign.}
The judge does not show a uniform ``longer $\to$ higher score'' bias:
the sign of the correlation depends
on the quality pattern of each model's responses.
The correlation is positive when longer responses
carry more valid reasoning
(both Qwen models in Direct mode),
and negative when longer responses
introduce more errors
(Gemini, and Qwen3-VL under CoT).
The Qwen3.5 Direct correlation of $+0.530$ is the strongest in the panel,
but the corresponding Q1$\to$Q4 progression (4.56 $\to$ 5.33)
shows that the longer-quartile responses
do score better in absolute terms,
indicating ``longer because more correct''
rather than ``higher score because longer.''

Switching to CoT generally eliminates or reverses the positive correlation:
Qwen3.5 drops from $+0.530$ to nearly zero,
and Qwen3-VL flips from $+0.394$ to $-0.241$.
This pattern is consistent
with the exposure effect identified in \Cref{sec:mathvista}:
under CoT, longer chains contain more visual assertions
and therefore more opportunities to be penalized,
so the judge becomes more sensitive
to noise in longer reasoning chains.

\subsection{Cross-Model Hallucination Consistency}

\textbf{Setup.}
This analysis examines whether hallucinations overlap across models
on the same questions.
For each sample,
the number of models that produce hallucinations (0--4) is counted.

\Cref{tab:cross_model_consistency} reports the distribution.

\begin{table}[htbp]

    \midrule

    VQA-RAD          & 451  & 59  & 118 & 122 & 81 & 71 & 33.7\% \\

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{fig5_radar_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{Radar chart comparing model performance
    across six dimensions (accuracy = $1 - \text{HR}$).
    Points closer to the outer ring indicate better performance.}
  \label{fig:radar_comparison}

\textbf{VQA-RAD difficulty is largely intrinsic.}
On VQA-RAD,
only 13.1\% of samples (59/451) are answered correctly by all four models,
and 33.7\% are answered incorrectly by three or more.
This concentration at the ``hard'' end of the spectrum
indicates a substantial pool of items
whose difficulty is intrinsic to the data
rather than specific to any individual model.
The finding sharpens the interpretation
of \Cref{sec:vqarad}:
the high VQA-RAD hallucination rates
are not driven by an idiosyncratic weakness of one or two models
but by a domain-level perception gap
shared across general-purpose MLLMs,
consistent with the ``knowledge $\neq$ perception'' diagnosis
of Case~2 (\Cref{sec:vqarad_case2}).

\textbf{MathVista and the polarization signature of CoT.}
MathVista Direct shows a more balanced distribution
(38.7\% fully correct, 6.8\% fully incorrect).
Under CoT the Hard Ratio rises from 11.7\% to 14.9\%,
a modest but consistent shift
in line with the CoT polarization effect
already documented in \Cref{sec:mathvista}:
CoT pushes responses toward the extremes
of either fully-correct or fully-wrong,
so the distribution at both tails grows
while intermediate cells shrink.
